<role>
The rules for choosing a document's reader and shaping the document, which
apply to any text longer than the short texts `SKILL.md` defines. Read this
file before you plan, write, edit or review a document. The rules in
`SKILL.md` still hold for every sentence.
</role>

<rules name="before you draft">
- E2. Decide who reads the document and state the level at the top of the
  draft. The same fact is written differently for each level, and a reviewer
  can check the level only if it's written down. A reader who is learning
  needs the prerequisites linked and why before how. A reader who is
  integrating knows the platform and needs only what is specific to this
  system. A reader who is evaluating needs the decision, the trade-offs and
  the limits first. Where the request names no reader, ask one question before
  you draft.
</rules>

<rules name="shape the document">
- E3. Give each paragraph one idea, topic sentence first, in three to five
  sentences, so a reader skimming first sentences still gets the argument.
- E4. Define a term before its first use, or gloss it in the same sentence,
  because a reader meeting an unknown name stops reading.
- E5. Open with the reader's problem in the reader's words and your answer to
  it, and bring in your own vocabulary once you have stated the problem,
  because a reader recognises their problem before they learn your names for
  it.
- E6. Open each section with its answer in two to four sentences, so a reader
  who stops after any section has a coherent picture.
- E7. Give each section one question. A section that answers two questions is
  two sections, and two sections that answer one question are one, because a
  reader looking for an answer scans the headings for one question each.
- E8. Give sibling sections the same shape, so the reader learns it once.
- E9. Keep the main path for what a first-time reader needs, and move numbers,
  edge cases, long tables and derivations to a section they can skip at the
  end, because every detail on the main path delays the reader who doesn't
  need it.
- E10. Refer back on the main path, never forward, apart from one pointer to
  the section E9 moves detail to: "section 9 explains why" means the sections
  are in the wrong order.
- E11. Refer to another section in a full sentence, never in a bare "Section
  8." fragment, because the reader needs to know why to follow the reference.
- E12. Add a glossary near the top when a document introduces more than five
  terms, so the reader has one place to look a term up.
- E13. Add a note on how to read the document only in a document over about
  3,000 words, because a shorter one shows its own order.
- E14. Put a table or figure beside the paragraph that uses it and say what the
  reader should take from it, because a reader who meets it elsewhere has to
  guess what it shows.
- E15. Test the order with a cold read: wherever a reader holding only the
  earlier sections meets a name they can't place, the structure has failed.
</rules>

<rules name="format a document">
- F1. Make the headings tell the story on their own, three levels at most, in
  sentence case with no end punctuation: a verb for a task, a noun phrase for a
  concept. A skimmer reads only the headings.
- F4. Bold at most one phrase per section, apart from the interface elements F3
  bolds, and open paragraphs in plain text, because a page of bold openers
  reads as orders nobody argued for.
</rules>
